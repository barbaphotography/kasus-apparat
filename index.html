Formular Nr. 4 · Case Determination

Der Kasus-Apparat

Four copies, four cases. Fill out the form correctly.

To figure out which case to use, ask yourself these 3 questions in order:
  1. Does the verb force a fixed case? (helfen, danken, gefallen → always dative)
  2. Is there a preposition? If it's fixed (für, mit, etc.) that preposition decides. If it's a two-way preposition (in, auf, an...), check for movement (accusative) or location (dative).
  3. If none of that applies: is it the subject (nominative) or the direct object (accusative)?
Nominative — the one acting

Wer? Was? (who? what?)

It's the subject of the sentence: whoever is doing the action, or whatever is being described with sein, werden, bleiben.

Der Hund bellt.
Who's barking? → der Hund → it's the subject → nominative.
Accusative — the one receiving the action

Wen? Was? (whom? what?)

It's the direct object: what receives the verb's action. Also required by certain fixed prepositions (durch, für, gegen, ohne, um) and by two-way prepositions when there's movement.

Ich sehe den Hund.
Whom do I see? → den Hund → direct object → accusative (der → den for masculine).
Dative — the recipient

Wem? (to/for whom?)

It's the indirect object: who something is being done to or for. English barely marks this (no difference between "I see him" and "I help him"), which is why it's the hardest to catch. Required by fixed verbs (helfen, danken, gefallen, gehören...), fixed prepositions (aus, bei, mit, nach, seit, von, zu), and two-way prepositions with location.

Ich helfe dem Mann.
"Helfen" always takes dative, regardless of subject/object logic → dem Mann.
Genitive — the possessive

Wessen? (whose?)

Shows possession. Barely used in spoken German — people say "von + dative" instead ("das Auto von dem Mann" instead of "das Auto des Mannes"). Still worth recognizing, especially with prepositions like wegen, während, trotz.

Das Auto des Mannes.
Whose car is it? → des Mannes → possession → genitive (der → des, plus "-es" on the noun).
Bonus — indefinite articles (ein/eine)

"a/an" instead of "the"

Everything above was about the definite article (der/die/das — "the"). The indefinite article (ein/eine — "a/an") follows the exact same case logic, just with different endings. Once you know der/die/das by case, this is nearly free: same endings (-em, -er, -en, -es), except nominative and accusative masculine/neuter drop the ending (just "ein", not "einer" or "eines").

Ich helfe einem Mann.
Same reasoning as before: "helfen" → dative → masculine dative is "-em" → dem becomes einem.

There's no indefinite plural — "eine Männer" doesn't exist. Plural uses no article, or "keine" to negate.
